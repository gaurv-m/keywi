The morning tide had left a line of shells along the shore. Mara walked beside it with an empty canvas bag, stopping whenever she found a shell with an unusual pattern. By the time she reached the old pier, she had collected enough to fill a small jar for her desk.

A narrow path connected the village to a sheltered bay. The first part climbed through a grove of palms, but the ground became level near the water. We followed it in the late afternoon and arrived just as the fishing boats were coming home.

Rain began while I was working in the garden. I carried the tools into the shed and watched the water gather on the broad leaves outside. When the shower passed, the soil was soft enough to plant the last row of seeds.

The library opened early on Saturdays. Sunlight crossed the reading tables, and the only sounds came from turning pages and a clock near the door. I chose a book about coastal journeys and spent the morning following its maps.

Our boat needed a fresh coat of paint before the summer trips began. We pulled it above the tide line, cleaned the hull, and waited for the surface to dry. The work took most of the day, but the finished boat looked ready for another season.

A cool breeze moved through the kitchen window while the bread was rising. I cleared the counter and set out a clean towel for the warm loaf. By noon, the room smelled of fresh bread, and lunch required little more than butter and a bowl of soup.

The footbridge had been closed for repairs since the last storm. Each morning, the carpenter replaced another damaged board and checked the railing. When the bridge finally opened, children crossed it slowly to look at the stream through the gaps.

We reached the lookout before sunrise and found a place beside the stone wall. At first, the valley was hidden beneath a flat layer of fog. As the sky brightened, the fog began to lift, revealing the river and the scattered roofs below.

The old bicycle had spent the winter in a corner of the shed. I pumped up the tires, cleaned the chain, and adjusted the loose brake cable. A short ride down the lane was enough to confirm that it was ready for the road again.

A neighbor gave us a small lemon tree in a clay pot. We placed it by the sunniest window and turned it a little each week so that it would grow evenly. Its first new leaves appeared before the end of the month.

The harbor market was busiest just after the boats arrived. Traders arranged fresh produce beside baskets of fish while customers made room for one another in the narrow aisles. We bought what we needed and carried it home along the quieter streets.

I found an unfinished sketch between the pages of an old notebook. It showed the garden gate before the climbing plants had covered it. That afternoon, I took the notebook outside and added the branches that had grown since the drawing began.

The train followed the coast for most of the journey. Every few minutes, a gap in the trees offered another view of the sea. We put away our books and watched the changing shoreline until the track turned inland.

A sudden gust scattered the papers on the picnic table. We gathered the loose sheets from the grass and held the stack beneath a smooth stone. The stone stayed there for the rest of the afternoon while we finished planning the route.

The first guests were due before sunset, so we carried the chairs into the courtyard. A long table fit neatly beneath the vines, leaving room for people to move around it. Once the lamps were lit, the empty space began to feel like a place for conversation.

The stream was shallow enough to cross on stepping stones. We tested each stone before moving our weight onto it and passed the bags across by hand. On the far bank, a dry patch of grass offered a good place to rest.

The small museum had a room devoted to local boatbuilders. Along one wall, photographs showed the same harbor changing over several generations. A guide pointed out the buildings that were still standing and explained how to find them from the main square.

Wind had carried sand over the lower section of the path. We brushed it away from the steps and marked a loose stone with a short branch. The next group of walkers thanked us as they passed on their way to the beach.

The workshop shelves were crowded with jars of screws and small pieces of wood. Before starting a new project, Ellis cleared a space near the window and laid out the tools in order. The simple preparation made it easier to work carefully through the afternoon.

We had planned to leave after breakfast, but a gentle rain kept us inside. Instead of packing the car, we spread the map across the floor and compared possible routes. By the time the weather cleared, we knew exactly where we wanted to stop.

The pond looked still from the garden wall. Closer to the edge, tiny rings appeared where insects touched the surface. I sat on the low bench and watched the rings spread until they disappeared among the reflections of the trees.

A parcel arrived with a roll of drawing paper and a set of pencils. I cleared the largest table in the room and unrolled the paper beneath a pair of books. The first marks were light, leaving plenty of room to change the shape of the drawing.

The trail divided beside a fallen tree. We checked the sign, chose the path toward the lake, and listened for the sound of water as we walked. Soon the trees opened, and a narrow beach appeared at the end of the trail.

Our tent faced a small clearing beneath the pines. In the morning, sunlight reached the clearing before it reached the campsite, so we carried our cups into the warm patch of grass. Breakfast could wait until the rest of the camp was awake.

The bakery on the corner had changed its opening hours. A handwritten note on the door explained that fresh loaves would be ready later than usual. We took a short walk around the square and returned just as the first trays reached the counter.

I was sorting photographs when I found a picture of our first visit to the island. The pier in the background was shorter, and the boat had a different name painted on its side. I placed the photograph beside a recent one to compare the changes.

A row of young trees stood beside the school fence. The students had planted them in spring and shared the work of watering them during dry weeks. By autumn, most of the trees had grown above the lowest rail.

The evening ferry was almost empty when we stepped aboard. We chose seats near the window and watched the harbor lights shrink behind us. The crossing was short, but it gave us enough time to finish the conversation we had started on the pier.

A shallow drawer held the seeds left over from earlier seasons. We read the labels, set aside the oldest packets, and made a list of what the garden still needed. Planning the beds on paper was easier than changing them after the first shoots appeared.

The path around the lake was longer than we remembered. We slowed our pace, found a bench in the shade, and shared the last of the water. After a short rest, the remaining distance felt quite manageable.

The curtains moved whenever the breeze reached the open window. I secured the loose corner with a small clip so that it would stop brushing against the desk. With the room quiet again, it was easy to return to the letter I had been writing.

A volunteer met us at the entrance to the nature reserve. She explained which paths were open and pointed to a board showing the birds seen that week. We chose a short route through the reeds and borrowed a pair of binoculars before setting out.
